At a glance

  • A decision tree is a set of nested if-else questions drawn as nodes. Each internal node splits the records on one feature, and splitting stops at pure leaves, where every record has the same class.
  • Purity is measured by entropy \(H = -p_+\log_2 p_+ - p_-\log_2 p_-\) (0 when pure, 1 at 50/50) or Gini impurity \(1 - \sum p_i^2\) (0 when pure, 0.5 at 50/50). Gini has no logarithm, so it is faster, and it is scikit-learn's default.
  • The feature to split on is the one with the largest information gain \(H(S) - \sum_v \frac{|S_v|}{|S|} H(S_v)\). His worked split, 9/5 into 6/2 and 3/3, gains about 0.048.
  • For a numerical feature, sort the values, try "\(\le v\)" at every value, and keep the threshold with the highest gain.
  • A regression tree predicts the mean of the records in a leaf and chooses splits by mean squared error (or mean absolute error).
  • A fully grown tree overfits. Post-pruning cuts useless branches afterwards; pre-pruning limits max_depth, max_leaf_nodes and similar hyperparameters, tuned with GridSearchCV. The practical fits DecisionTreeClassifier on Iris and draws it with tree.plot_tree.

Pure and impure splits

A node is pure when all its records have the same output: all yes and zero no, or zero yes and all no. Overcast, with 4 yes and 0 no, is pure. If day 15 is overcast, the tree already knows the answer is yes, so there is no reason to split this node further. A pure node is a leaf node.

Sunny, with 2 yes and 3 no, is impure. The tree takes another feature (he suggests Temperature, as an example) and splits the Sunny records again. Rain is treated the same way. Splitting continues until every branch ends in a pure leaf.

This raises two questions, and the rest of the chapter answers them.

  1. How do we measure purity? By eye, counting yes and no is enough, but the algorithm needs a number. Two are used: entropy and Gini impurity. (He first calls it the Gini coefficient and corrects himself to Gini impurity.)
  2. Which feature should be split first? Why Outlook and not Wind? This is decided by information gain.

Purity decides when a branch stops. Information gain decides which feature a node splits on.

Entropy: a number for impurity

He writes both purity formulas side by side, then works through entropy first.

Entropy of a sample S (two classes) \[ H(S) = -\,p_+ \log_2 p_+ \;-\; p_- \log_2 p_- \]

\(p_+\) is the probability of yes in the node (the fraction of its records that are yes) and \(p_-\) the probability of no. Plus and minus stand for the two classes of a binary problem, such as yes/no or positive/negative.

His example: a root node on feature 1 holds 6 yes and 3 no. The feature has two categories, and the split sends 3 yes, 3 no to C1 and 3 yes, 0 no to C2. Again the children add up to the root: \(3 + 3 = 6\) yes, \(3 + 0 = 3\) no.

\[ \begin{aligned} H(C_2) &= -\tfrac33\log_2\tfrac33 - \tfrac03\log_2\tfrac03 = -1\cdot 0 - 0 = 0 \\ H(C_1) &= -\tfrac36\log_2\tfrac36 - \tfrac36\log_2\tfrac36 = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1 \end{aligned} \]

\(\log_2 1 = 0\), and the \(0 \log_2 0\) term is taken as 0.

  • C2 is a pure split, and its entropy is 0. Whenever a node is pure, the entropy comes out as zero.
  • C1 has entropy 1. With 3 yes and 3 no the chance of either answer is 50%, the most impure a two-class node can be.

Plotting \(H\) against \(p_+\) gives the curve below. Because \(p_- = 1 - p_+\), one number fixes the node. At \(p_+ = 0.5\) the entropy is 1; at \(p_+ = 0\) or \(p_+ = 1\) the node is pure and the entropy is 0. In between it is somewhere from 0 to 1, read off the curve. So for two classes the entropy always lies between 0 and 1.

This is the purity test done at every split. If a child comes out impure, the tree takes another feature and divides that child further.

Not in the video: for \(K\) classes the general form is \(H = -\sum_{i=1}^{K} p_i \log_2 p_i\), and its maximum is \(\log_2 K\), so the 0-to-1 range holds only for two classes. For the IIT Madras treatment of entropy as an impurity measure, see Part 13 (part-13.html).

Information gain: which feature to split on

Now the second question. The data has features \(F_1, F_2, F_3, \dots\). Feature 1 might have two categories and feature 2 three. The same choice comes up again lower down, because every impure child needs another feature. How does the tree decide which feature to split on first? It computes the information gain of the split each feature would make.

Information gain of splitting sample S on feature F \[ \text{Gain}(S, F) = H(S) - \sum_{v \,\in\, \text{Values}(F)} \frac{|S_v|}{|S|}\, H(S_v) \]

\(H(S)\) is the entropy of the node being split (the root, here). \(S_v\) is the child holding the records with category \(v\); \(|S_v|/|S|\) is the fraction of the records that went there, and \(H(S_v)\) is that child's entropy.

His worked split

Feature 1 splits a root of 9 yes, 5 no (14 records) into C1 with 6 yes, 2 no (8 records) and C2 with 3 yes, 3 no (6 records).

  1. Gain(S, F1), step by step
  2. Entropy of the root: \(H(S) = -\tfrac{9}{14}\log_2\tfrac{9}{14} - \tfrac{5}{14}\log_2\tfrac{5}{14} \approx 0.94\).
  3. Entropy of C1: \(H(S_{C_1}) = -\tfrac68\log_2\tfrac68 - \tfrac28\log_2\tfrac28 \approx 0.81\).
  4. Entropy of C2: 3 yes and 3 no, so \(H(S_{C_2}) = 1\).
  5. Weights: C1 holds 8 of the 14 records and C2 holds 6, so the weights are \(\tfrac{8}{14}\) and \(\tfrac{6}{14}\).
  6. \(\text{Gain}(S, F_1) = 0.94 - \tfrac{8}{14}(0.81) - \tfrac{6}{14}(1) = 0.9403 - 0.4636 - 0.4286 \approx 0.048\).

The captions give his final answer as 0.041; the arithmetic gives 0.048. The ranking argument below does not change.

Suppose the same calculation for feature 2 gives \(\text{Gain}(S, F_2) = 0.51\). Since \(\text{Gain}(S, F_2) > \text{Gain}(S, F_1)\), the tree starts the split with feature 2. In general it computes the gain of every candidate feature and takes the one with the highest information gain, at the root and again at every node below it.

  1. Growing a classification tree
  2. At the current node, compute its entropy (or Gini impurity).
  3. For every candidate feature, split the node's records by the feature's categories and compute the information gain.
  4. Split on the feature with the largest gain: one child per category.
  5. A pure child becomes a leaf. An impure child goes back to step 1 with only its own records.

Not in the video: his feature-1 counts (9/5 into 6/2 and 3/3) are exactly the Wind column of the play-tennis table. Computing all four features shows why the classic tree puts Outlook at the root, the feature he started with.

Gini impurity

Entropy works, so why Gini? Gini impurity measures the same thing with simpler arithmetic.

Gini impurity \[ \text{Gini} = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i^2 \;\overset{\text{two classes}}{=}\; 1 - \big(p_+^2 + p_-^2\big) \]

\(n\) is the number of output classes; here two, yes and no.

For a node with 2 yes and 2 no, both probabilities are \(\tfrac12\):

\[ \text{Gini} = 1 - \Big[\big(\tfrac12\big)^2 + \big(\tfrac12\big)^2\Big] = 1 - \big(\tfrac14 + \tfrac14\big) = \tfrac12 = 0.5 \]

This node is completely impure. For such a node entropy gives 1 and Gini gives 0.5. That is the difference between the two curves in the figure above: the Gini curve has the same shape, 0 at a pure node, but its peak at \(p_+ = 0.5\) is 0.5. Scikit-learn's decision tree classifier uses Gini impurity by default 3:33:17.

Later a viewer asks whether a Gini value can reach 1 4:02:28. His answer: no, it stays between 0 and 0.5.

Correction: that range holds for two classes. With \(K\) equally likely classes, Gini is \(1 - K\cdot\tfrac{1}{K^2} = 1 - \tfrac1K\), so 0.667 for three classes. The Iris tree in the practical shows exactly this value at its root, which puzzles him there.

Not in the video: with Gini the split score is computed the same way as information gain, the parent's Gini minus the size-weighted Gini of the children. The calculator above shows both.

3:51:40 3:52:45 the log costs time

Gini or entropy?

His deciding factor is execution time. Decision trees are expensive to train: with 100 features the tree compares split after split and computes an information gain for each. Entropy needs a logarithm for every term, while Gini needs only squares. So entropy takes more time, and Gini is faster.

  • Many features (100 or 200): use Gini impurity.
  • A small set of features: entropy is fine.

Not in the video: because the two curves have the same shape, they usually pick the same or very similar splits, so the choice rarely changes accuracy much.

Numerical features: try every threshold

So far every feature was categorical, and a split meant one child per category. A numerical feature \(F_1\) has no categories, so the tree turns it into a yes/no question \(F_1 \le v\). His example values are 2.3, 1.3, 4, 5, 7 and 3.

  1. Choosing a threshold for a numerical feature
  2. Sort the values: 1.3, 2.3, 3, 4, 5, 7.
  3. Take the first value and ask \(F_1 \le 1.3\)? One record goes to the yes branch (a single record is already a leaf) and the rest to the no branch. Count the yes and no outputs on each side and compute the information gain.
  4. Take the first two records: \(F_1 \le 2.3\)? Two records go to yes and the rest to no. Compute the gain again.
  5. Continue with \(F_1 \le 3\), \(F_1 \le 4\), …, once for every record.
  6. Keep the threshold with the highest information gain and split the node there.

He does not give the output labels for these values, so the figure assigns illustrative ones and computes the gains.

Decision tree regression: split by mean squared error

In a decision tree regressor the output is a continuous variable and can take any value. The tree still splits on features; what changes is what a node predicts and how a split is scored.

  • A node predicts the mean of the outputs of its records. Before any split, the mean of the whole output column is assigned to the root.
  • The cost function is mean squared error, or mean absolute error, instead of entropy or Gini impurity.

He recalls the mean squared error from linear regression (Part 1), with \(\hat y\) now the node's mean:

\[ \hat y_\text{node} = \bar y = \frac1n \sum_{i \in \text{node}} y_i, \qquad \text{MSE}(\text{node}) = \frac1n \sum_{i \in \text{node}} \big(y_i - \bar y\big)^2 \]

He writes it in his linear-regression form, \(\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\hat y_i - y_i)^2\). The constant in front does not change which split is best; these notes use \(\frac1n\).

The procedure: assign the mean and compute the MSE at the node. Split on a feature; some records go to each child, each child gets its own mean, and the MSE is computed again in each child. As the MSE falls, the nodes get closer to leaves. A new record follows its path down the tree, and the mean value in the leaf it reaches is the prediction.

Not in the video: the split score, written out \[ \Delta = \text{MSE}(S) - \sum_{v} \frac{|S_v|}{|S|}\, \text{MSE}(S_v) \]

The same shape as information gain, with MSE in place of entropy. MSE around the mean is the variance of the node's outputs, so \(\Delta\) is also called variance reduction. The tree picks the split with the largest \(\Delta\).

His example

A viewer asks for a regression example, so he sketches one: feature F1 and outputs 20, 24, 26, 28, 30. The root gets their mean and an MSE (he names a rough placeholder value without computing it). After a split, two of the records go to one child, the means change and the MSE is calculated again. The numbers below are computed for these notes, with F1 given the illustrative values 1 to 5.

Not in the video: in scikit-learn this is DecisionTreeRegressor, whose default criterion='squared_error' is the MSE above; criterion='absolute_error' uses MAE, and its leaves then predict the median instead of the mean.

Overfitting, post-pruning and pre-pruning

Before the regression example he poses a question: what happens if the tree is allowed to grow to any depth? The answer comes after it. A decision tree keeps dividing nodes to whatever level we allow, until the leaves are pure, so a fully grown tree overfits. It fits the training data, noise included. There are two ways to prevent it.

Post-pruning

Build the whole tree first, then look at it and cut the branches that are not needed. His example: a node holds 7 yes and 2 no and is split further. The node already says yes for most of its records (7 of 9 is about 78%, which he rounds up to more than 80%), so splitting it more adds little. Cut the branch there and make the node a leaf.

Pre-pruning

Decide the limits before the tree is grown, through hyperparameters: the maximum depth, the maximum number of leaf nodes, and similar settings. Try combinations with GridSearchCV and keep the best.

For the IIT Madras treatment of stopping rules and tree depth as a hyperparameter chosen by validation, see Part 13 (part-13.html). Not in the video: scikit-learn's built-in post-pruning is cost-complexity pruning through ccp_alpha, used in the last practical section.

Reading the tree, and pruning it by eye

Each box of the plot shows the split question, the Gini impurity, the number of samples and the class counts (value). The figure redraws the top four levels of the tree he gets.

What he points out:

  • The left branch of the root is a leaf. One feature alone separates 50 flowers into a pure node. He calls it versicolor; it is setosa, class 0, as value = [50, 0, 0] shows.
  • The right branch holds the other two classes 50/50, so it is split again on another feature, and then again (the petal length ≤ 4.95 box).
  • Post-pruning by eye. At the box with value = [0, 47, 1] he asks whether any more splitting is needed. It is not: the node is almost pure, and the split below it only isolates one flower. The same holds after the [0, 1, 45] box. Looking at the drawn tree like this, you can decide the level at which to stop.

He is surprised that the root shows gini = 0.667, since he expects Gini never to exceed 0.5, and says he will look into it. Everywhere else in the tree the value is below 0.5.

The explanation: Iris has three classes. With 50 of each at the root, \(\text{Gini} = 1 - 3\cdot\big(\tfrac13\big)^2 = \tfrac23 \approx 0.667\). The 0-to-0.5 range is for two classes only. Every node below the root contains at most two of the three classes, so its Gini stays at or below 0.5.

Not in the video: tuning and evaluating the tree

The practical fits the tree on all 150 flowers and never measures accuracy. Pre-pruning with GridSearchCV is described only in the theory. This section adds the standard next steps, so the practical can be finished in code. The outputs in the comments come from running this code with scikit-learn 1.6.1 for these notes.

Hold out a test set and see the overfit

from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split, GridSearchCV
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score, confusion_matrix, classification_report

X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    iris.data, iris.target, test_size=0.33, random_state=42)

full = DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0).fit(X_train, y_train)
full.get_depth(), full.get_n_leaves()                       # (6, 10)
full.score(X_train, y_train), full.score(X_test, y_test)    # (1.0, 0.96)

The unpruned tree classifies every training flower correctly (accuracy 1.0) and 96% of the 50 test flowers.

Pre-pruning: grid search over the hyperparameters

params = {
    'criterion': ['gini', 'entropy'],
    'max_depth': [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, None],
    'max_leaf_nodes': [None, 3, 4, 5, 6],
}
grid = GridSearchCV(DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0), params, cv=5, scoring='accuracy')
grid.fit(X_train, y_train)
grid.best_params_    # {'criterion': 'gini', 'max_depth': 3, 'max_leaf_nodes': None}
grid.best_score_     # 0.93  (mean accuracy over the 5 validation folds)

y_pred = grid.predict(X_test)        # uses the best tree, refitted on all of X_train
accuracy_score(y_test, y_pred)       # 0.98
confusion_matrix(y_test, y_pred)     # [[19  0  0]
                                     #  [ 0 15  0]
                                     #  [ 0  1 15]]
print(classification_report(y_test, y_pred, target_names=iris.target_names))

The search settles on a depth-3 tree with 5 leaves. It misclassifies one virginica as versicolor and scores 0.98 on the test set. Several settings tie at 0.93 in cross-validation; GridSearchCV keeps the first. With only 50 test flowers, one flower is 2 points of accuracy, so read small differences with care.

Post-pruning: cost-complexity pruning

Scikit-learn automates post-pruning. cost_complexity_pruning_path lists the values of ccp_alpha at which branches of the full tree get cut; a larger alpha prunes more.

path.ccp_alphas      # [0.     0.0094 0.0097 0.0338 0.254  0.3209]

for a in path.ccp_alphas:
    m = DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0, ccp_alpha=a).fit(X_train, y_train)

Key terms

Decision tree
Nested if-else questions drawn as nodes; each internal node splits on one feature and each leaf gives the answer.
Root node
The first split, holding the whole training set (9 yes, 5 no in the play-tennis data).
Leaf node
A node that is not split further. It predicts its majority class, or its mean in regression.
Pure and impure split
Pure: every record in the node has the same output, such as 4 yes, 0 no. Impure: mixed, so the node is split again.
Entropy
\(H(S) = -p_+\log_2 p_+ - p_-\log_2 p_-\): 0 for a pure node, 1 for a 50/50 node.
Gini impurity
\(1 - \sum p_i^2\): 0 for a pure node, 0.5 at 50/50 with two classes (\(1 - 1/K\) with \(K\) classes). Scikit-learn's default.
Information gain
\(H(S) - \sum_v \frac{|S_v|}{|S|}H(S_v)\): how much a split lowers entropy. The feature with the largest gain is chosen.
Threshold split
For a numerical feature, the question \(F \le v\); every sorted value is tried and the best gain wins.
Decision tree regressor
A tree with a continuous output: each leaf predicts the mean of its records, and splits are scored by MSE or MAE.
Variance reduction
The drop from the parent's MSE to the size-weighted MSE of its children; the regression analogue of information gain.
Post-pruning
Grow the full tree, then cut branches that add nothing (by eye in the video; ccp_alpha in scikit-learn).
Pre-pruning
Limit growth in advance with max_depth, max_leaf_nodes, min_samples_leaf…, tuned with GridSearchCV.
plot_tree
sklearn.tree.plot_tree(model, filled=True) draws a fitted tree with each node's split, impurity, samples and class counts.

Q1What is the entropy of a node with 4 yes and 4 no? And of a node with 4 yes and 0 no?

4 yes, 4 no: \(p_+ = p_- = \tfrac12\), so \(H = -\tfrac12\log_2\tfrac12 - \tfrac12\log_2\tfrac12 = 1\), the most impure two-class node. 4 yes, 0 no: \(H = -1\cdot\log_2 1 - 0 = 0\), a pure leaf.

Q2A node has 3 yes and 1 no. Compute its Gini impurity and its entropy.

\(p_+ = 0.75\), \(p_- = 0.25\). Gini \(= 1 - (0.5625 + 0.0625) = 0.375\). Entropy \(= -0.75\log_2 0.75 - 0.25\log_2 0.25 = 0.311 + 0.5 \approx 0.811\). Both are between their pure value (0) and their 50/50 peak (0.5 and 1).

Q3Compute the information gain of splitting the play-tennis root (9 yes, 5 no) on Outlook.

\(H(S) = 0.940\). Sunny (2, 3) and Rain (3, 2) each have entropy 0.971 and hold 5 of 14 records; Overcast (4, 0) has entropy 0. Weighted child entropy \(= \tfrac{5}{14}(0.971) + \tfrac{4}{14}(0) + \tfrac{5}{14}(0.971) = 0.694\), so the gain is \(0.940 - 0.694 = 0.247\), the largest of the four features.

Q4When does he recommend Gini impurity over entropy, and why?

When there are many features (100 or 200). Training compares a great many candidate splits, and entropy needs a logarithm in every term while Gini needs only squares, so Gini is faster. With few features entropy is fine.

Q5In one node a numerical feature takes the values 9, 2, 5, 8, 5. Which thresholds does his procedure try, and how many different splits do they give?

Sort: 2, 5, 5, 8, 9. The questions are \(\le 2\), \(\le 5\), \(\le 8\) and \(\le 9\) (the repeated 5 gives the same question twice). \(\le 9\) sends every record to the yes side, so only 3 are real splits: one fewer than the number of distinct values. Each is scored by information gain and the best is kept.

Q6A regression-tree leaf holds the outputs 10, 12 and 14. What does it predict for a new record that reaches it, and what is its MSE?

It predicts the mean, 12. MSE \(= \tfrac13\big[(10-12)^2 + 0^2 + (14-12)^2\big] = \tfrac83 \approx 2.67\).

Q7The Iris tree's root shows gini = 0.667, yet he says Gini lies between 0 and 0.5. Who is right?

Both, in their own setting. The 0 to 0.5 range is for two classes. The Iris root has three classes with 50 flowers each, so \(\text{Gini} = 1 - 3(\tfrac13)^2 = \tfrac23\). In general the maximum is \(1 - 1/K\) for \(K\) classes.

Q8What is the difference between pre-pruning and post-pruning, and how is each done in scikit-learn?

Pre-pruning limits the tree before it grows: set max_depth, max_leaf_nodes, min_samples_leaf and similar, choosing them with GridSearchCV. Post-pruning grows the full tree first and then removes branches that add little. The video does it by inspecting plot_tree (cutting below the [0, 47, 1] node, for example); scikit-learn's tool for it is ccp_alpha.
