At a glance

  • An ensemble uses several models on one problem. Bagging trains them in parallel, each on its own sample of rows, and combines them by majority vote for classification or by the mean for regression.
  • Boosting chains weak learners one after another. Each concentrates on the records the previous one got wrong, and together they make a strong learner.
  • A fully grown decision tree has low bias and high variance. A random forest bags many such trees, each on sampled rows and features, and the vote turns the high variance into low variance.
  • A random forest needs no feature scaling and is not much affected by outliers. KNN needs scaling and is affected by outliers, because it works with distances.
  • One AdaBoost round: equal weights \(1/n\), a one-split stump, total error \(\mathit{TE}\) = weight of the wrong records, performance \(\alpha = \tfrac12\ln\frac{1-\mathit{TE}}{\mathit{TE}}\), wrong weights times \(e^{\alpha}\) and correct ones times \(e^{-\alpha}\), normalize, then resample through buckets for the next stump.

What an ensemble is

Every algorithm so far in the video (linear and logistic regression, Naive Bayes, KNN, decision trees) solved a classification or regression problem on its own, one model at a time. This part opens with a new question: can several algorithms work on one problem together? They can, and the methods that do this are called ensemble techniques.

There are two ways to combine models, bagging and boosting. The session explains both, then the random forest (a bagging method) and AdaBoost (a boosting method). XGBoost was also on the agenda; it comes later in the video, in Part 8.

Bagging: parallel models and a vote

Start with a data set \(D\): features \(f_1, f_2, f_3, \dots\), an output column, and say 10,000 rows. Bagging sets up several models \(M_1, M_2, M_3, M_4, \dots\). In general they can be of any type. Krish's example mixes logistic regression, a decision tree, KNN and a second decision tree.

Each model trains on a row sample of \(D\): some of the rows (data points), with all the features. If \(M_1\) gets 1,000 of the 10,000 rows, call that sample \(D'\). \(M_2\) gets another sample \(D''\), \(M_3\) gets \(D'''\), and so on. Each sample is smaller than \(D\), and a row can turn up in more than one sample. So every model learns from slightly different data.

Combining the outputs

A new test point goes to every model. Take a binary classification where \(M_1\) says 0 and \(M_2\), \(M_3\) and \(M_4\) say 1. The outputs are aggregated by majority voting: three models say 1, so the answer is 1. Training on row samples and then aggregating gives bagging its other name, bootstrap aggregation (Krish calls the combining step the bootstrap aggregator).

What if half the models say 0 and half say 1? In practice a bagging model has 100 to 200 or more models, so an exact tie is very unlikely.

For regression the aggregate is the mean. If the four models predict 120, 140, 122 and 148, the bagged prediction is \((120 + 140 + 122 + 148)/4 = 132.5\).

The random forest, coming next, uses only decision trees. Bagging as an idea allows any mix of models.

Not in the video: the textbook bootstrap draws \(n\) rows with replacement, as many as \(D\) has, so a row can repeat even inside one sample. Each sample then holds about 63% of the distinct rows. This is what scikit-learn's bagging and random forest do by default.

Boosting: weak learners in sequence

In bagging the models sit side by side, and none depends on another. Boosting puts them in a sequence. The training data goes to \(M_1\), then on to \(M_2\), \(M_3\) and \(M_4\), and the output comes out at the end.

Each model in the chain is a weak learner, a simple model whose predictions on their own are poor. Combined in sequence they become a strong learner. Krish's analogy: think of \(M_1\) to \(M_4\) as teachers of physics, chemistry, maths and geography. If the physics teacher cannot solve a problem, one of the others may manage it, and together they cover far more than any one of them.

Interview answer: boosting combines a sequence of models, usually weak learners, into one strong learner.

Bagging and boosting methods are behind many Kaggle competition solutions. Someone in the chat asks whether every model sees all the features; Krish answers that in the random forest section, under feature sampling.

4:21:29 4:22:23 why not just prune

Why one decision tree is not enough

Recall the main problem with a decision tree (Part 5). Grown without any hyperparameters, it keeps splitting on the features until it reaches full depth, and it overfits: accuracy is high on the training data and low on the test data.

In Krish's terms, high training accuracy means low bias and low test accuracy means high variance. (He first says "high bias" and corrects himself.) A model that generalizes should have low bias and low variance.

Pruning can fix an overfit tree, but it is a lot of work on a big problem, say 100 features and a million data points. Pre-pruning, which limits the tree before it grows, is possible too, with no guarantee that it works well. The random forest attacks the variance in a different way.

Goal: keep the low bias of deep trees and convert their high variance into low variance.

Bias and variance get their own chapter in Part 8. For the IIT Madras view of the trade-off, see Part 17 (part-17.html).

Random forest: bagged decision trees

A random forest is a bagging technique in which every model is a decision tree. There are many trees \(M_1, M_2, M_3, M_4, \dots\) and no other kind of model.

Each tree gets its data through two kinds of sampling:

  • Row sampling: a sample of the records, as in bagging.
  • Feature sampling: a subset of the columns. With four features, one tree might get two of them, another three, another all four, and another just one. The random forest chooses these internally.

Rows and features can repeat from one tree to the next. Trained on its own slice of the data, each tree becomes an expert on that slice, like the subject teachers in the boosting analogy.

To predict, pass the test point to every tree. With outputs 0, 1, 0 and 0, the majority vote gives 0. The random forest regressor differs only in the last step: it averages the trees' outputs.

Why the vote reduces variance

Each fully grown tree has low bias and high variance: it fits the quirks of its own sample. The trees see different rows and features, so their quirks differ, and in a vote over many trees they largely cancel. What survives is the pattern most trees agree on. The forest keeps the low bias of its trees and has much lower variance, which is the generalized model the previous section asked for.

Not in the video: if \(M\) trees each have variance \(\sigma^2\) and every pair has correlation \(\rho\), their average has variance \(\rho\sigma^2 + (1-\rho)\,\sigma^2/M\). Adding trees shrinks only the second term. Feature sampling makes the trees less alike, which lowers \(\rho\) and with it the first term.

Not in the video: in Breiman's random forest, and in scikit-learn, the feature subset is drawn afresh at every split, not once per tree. For classification the usual size is about \(\sqrt{d}\) of the \(d\) features; scikit-learn's regressor uses all features unless you set max_features.

Not in the video · the same ideas in scikit-learn

from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier, RandomForestRegressor

rf = RandomForestClassifier(
    n_estimators=100,      # number of decision trees
    bootstrap=True,        # row sampling: each tree gets n rows drawn with replacement
    max_features="sqrt",   # feature sampling: about sqrt(d) features tried at each split
    random_state=0,
)
rf.fit(X_train, y_train)
rf.predict(X_test)         # averages the trees' class probabilities (a soft majority vote)

rfr = RandomForestRegressor(n_estimators=100, random_state=0)
rfr.fit(X_train, y_train)
rfr.predict(X_test)        # mean of the trees' predictions

Interview questions on random forests

Krish lists four questions that come up in interviews, two about the random forest and the same two about KNN for contrast.

QuestionRandom forest or decision treeKNN
Is normalization or standardization required?NoYes
Is it impacted by outliers?NoYes

Scaling. A tree only asks whether a feature is above or below a threshold. Shrinking or stretching a feature moves the threshold along with the data, so the same rows land on each side and the splits stay the same. KNN uses Euclidean or Manhattan distance, and a feature with a large range dominates that distance, so the features have to be standardized first (KNN is in Part 4).

Outliers. Krish gives the answers, no for the random forest and yes for KNN, and suggests looking up the reasons. Not in the video: a split depends only on the order of the values, so an extreme feature value just lands in the outermost branch and moves at most one threshold; it also appears in only some of the trees' samples, and the vote dilutes it further. KNN predicts straight from the nearest training points, so with a small \(k\) one unusual neighbour can swing the answer. (An extreme target value in a regression forest still pulls the mean of the leaf it falls into.)

In bagging the random forest is the usual choice. You can also build a custom bagging model by hand from any combination of algorithms you like.

AdaBoost: sample weights and the first stump

AdaBoost is the first boosting algorithm of the session. Krish solves a classification problem with it, on a data set with four features \(f_1, \dots, f_4\), a yes/no output and 7 records.

Step 0: sample weights. Every record gets a sample weight between 0 and 1. At the start they are all equal and add up to 1, so with 7 records each weight is \(1/7 \approx 0.143\).

Step 1: a stump. Choose the feature to split on the way a decision tree does, by information gain with entropy or Gini impurity (Part 5). Say \(f_1\) wins. Build a tree on it with depth one, a single split. A one-level tree is called a stump, and stumps are AdaBoost's weak learners.

Train the stump on all the records, then pass the records through it to see which ones it gets right. In the example it gets one record wrong, record 4, and the other six right.

4:34:20 4:34:53 performance of stump

Total error and the performance of the stump

Step 2: total error. Add up the sample weights of the records the stump misclassified. Only record 4 is wrong, so

\[ \mathit{TE} = \sum_{i \,\in\, \text{wrong}} w_i = \frac17 \approx 0.143 \]

Step 3: performance of the stump. Krish calls this quantity the performance of stump (PS). It is usually written \(\alpha\).

Performance of a stump \[ \alpha = \frac12 \ln\frac{1 - \mathit{TE}}{\mathit{TE}}, \qquad \text{here} \quad \alpha = \frac12 \ln\frac{6/7}{1/7} = \frac12\ln 6 \approx 0.896 \]

Krish reads the value out as 0.895. The captions render the factor in front as "1 by log e"; it is one half times the natural logarithm.

The fewer mistakes a stump makes, the larger its \(\alpha\), which is why it measures the stump's performance.

Not in the video: a stump with \(\mathit{TE} = 0.5\) is a coin flip and gets \(\alpha = 0\). Above 0.5, \(\alpha\) turns negative.

4:36:01 4:37:02 the two formulas 4:37:35 the numbers

Updating the sample weights

Step 4: new sample weights. Total error and \(\alpha\) exist to change the weights so that the next weak learner pays attention to the mistakes. The weights of correctly classified records go down and the weights of misclassified records go up. A record with a bigger weight is more likely to be passed on to the next stump.

\[ \text{correct record: } \; w \leftarrow w \cdot e^{-\alpha}, \qquad \text{wrong record: } \; w \leftarrow w \cdot e^{+\alpha} \]

In the example, with \(\alpha \approx 0.895\):

  • each correct record: \(\tfrac17 \cdot e^{-0.895} \approx 0.058\), which Krish writes as 0.05;
  • the wrong record 4: \(\tfrac17 \cdot e^{0.895} \approx 0.349\).

So the six correct records fall from 0.143 to about 0.05, and record 4 rises from 0.143 to 0.349.

Normalizing the weights

The updated weights no longer add up to 1. With Krish's rounded numbers the sum is \(6 \times 0.05 + 0.349 = 0.649\). Step 5: normalize by dividing every weight by that sum, so the weights add up to 1 again:

\[ w_i \leftarrow \frac{w_i}{\sum_j w_j}: \qquad \frac{0.05}{0.649} \approx 0.077, \qquad \frac{0.349}{0.649} \approx 0.537 \]

Not in the video: the mistakes always end up with half the weight

With \(e^{\alpha} = \sqrt{(1-\mathit{TE})/\mathit{TE}}\), the wrong records' total after the update is \(\mathit{TE} \cdot e^{\alpha} = \sqrt{\mathit{TE}(1-\mathit{TE})}\), and the correct records' total is \((1-\mathit{TE})\,e^{-\alpha} = \sqrt{\mathit{TE}(1-\mathit{TE})}\). The two are equal, so

\[ \sum_j w_j = 2\sqrt{\mathit{TE}\,(1-\mathit{TE})}, \qquad \text{share of the wrong records after normalizing} = \tfrac12 \]

Here \(2\sqrt{(1/7)(6/7)} = 2\sqrt6/7 \approx 0.700\). On the new weights the stump just trained has a total error of exactly 0.5, a coin flip, so the next stump has to find something new.

Buckets: choosing the next stump's records

The second stump in the sequence needs its own training data, and that data should contain mostly the records the first stump got wrong, along with some of the others. AdaBoost builds it from the normalized weights by making buckets: consecutive ranges on the interval from 0 to 1, one per record, each as wide as that record's weight. Record 1's bucket runs from 0 to 0.077, record 2's from 0.077 to 0.154, and so on; record 4's runs from 0.231 to 0.768 (the last column of the table above).

Then generate random numbers between 0 and 1. Each number falls into one bucket, and that bucket's record goes into the new data set. Record 4 owns more than half of the interval, so it is picked most often; the correct records still turn up now and then. If four or five records had been wrong, all their buckets would be wide and together they would fill most of the new sample.

Krish does not say how many records to draw. The usual choice is as many as the original data set has, here 7.

Train stump 2 on the new data set. It makes its own mistakes, their weights are raised in the same way, and the next resample goes to stump 3. A full model chains many stumps, 100 or more, so it takes longer to train than a single tree.

  1. AdaBoost, as Krish runs it
  2. Give each of the \(n\) records the weight \(w_i = 1/n\).
  3. Pick a feature by information gain (entropy or Gini), build a stump on it, train it, and find which records it misclassifies.
  4. Total error: \(\mathit{TE} = \sum_{\text{wrong}} w_i\).
  5. Performance of the stump: \(\alpha = \tfrac12\ln\frac{1-\mathit{TE}}{\mathit{TE}}\).
  6. Update: \(w_i \leftarrow w_i\,e^{-\alpha}\) for correct records and \(w_i \leftarrow w_i\,e^{\alpha}\) for wrong ones.
  7. Normalize: divide every weight by the sum of the weights.
  8. Make buckets from the normalized weights and draw random numbers between 0 and 1 to build the next stump's data set.
  9. Repeat from step 2 on the new data set. To predict, combine the outputs of all the stumps.

4:44:09 4:45:45 every stump counts

The final prediction

For a new test point, every stump gives an output. In a binary problem they might say 0, 1, 1 and 1; Krish combines them by majority vote, which gives 1. For regression he averages the outputs.

Someone asks why boosting does not simply take the last stump's output. Krish's answer is that every stump's output is important, so all of them are combined.

Not in the video: the standard vote is weighted by α

In standard AdaBoost each stump's vote counts in proportion to its performance \(\alpha_t\). With the classes coded \(-1\) and \(+1\):

\[ H(\mathbf{x}) = \operatorname{sign}\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{T} \alpha_t\, h_t(\mathbf{x})\Big) \]

A stump with a small total error has a large \(\alpha_t\) and a big say; a stump near a coin flip has \(\alpha_t \approx 0\) and barely counts. scikit-learn's AdaBoost regressor combines its trees with a weighted median rather than a plain average.

Not in the video · AdaBoost with stumps in scikit-learn

from sklearn.ensemble import AdaBoostClassifier
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier

ada = AdaBoostClassifier(
    estimator=DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=1),  # a stump (also the default)
    n_estimators=100,                                # number of stumps in the sequence
    random_state=0,
)
ada.fit(X_train, y_train)   # reweights the records each round instead of resampling
ada.predict(X_test)         # weighted vote of the stumps

scikit-learn passes the weights straight to the tree instead of drawing buckets. Its formula leaves out the ½ and multiplies only the wrong records' weights, by \((1-\mathit{TE})/\mathit{TE}\); after normalizing this gives the same weights as the update above, and the same vote. White-box and black-box models

Before moving on, Krish contrasts two kinds of model. In a white-box model you can follow how a prediction is made. In a black-box model you cannot.

Linear regressionwhite boxYou can watch \(\theta\) change as gradient descent moves to the global minimum.
Decision treewhite boxThe splits can be traced with paper and pen.
Random forestblack boxThere are too many trees to follow how each one decides; the maths inside is complex.
Artificial neural networkblack boxYou cannot see what the many neurons do or how their weights get updated.

Key terms

Ensemble technique
Several models used together on one problem. Bagging and boosting are the two kinds.
Bagging (bootstrap aggregation)
Models trained in parallel on row samples, combined by majority vote or by the mean.
Row sampling
Giving each model a sample of the records; rows can repeat across models.
Feature sampling
Giving each tree a subset of the columns; libraries draw a fresh subset at every split.
Majority voting
The class predicted by most models wins. Regression takes the mean instead.
Boosting
Weak learners trained one after another, each focusing on the previous one's mistakes.
Weak and strong learner
A weak learner predicts poorly on its own; many weak learners combined make a strong learner.
Random forest
Bagging with decision trees only, using row and feature sampling, to turn high variance into low variance.
Decision stump
A decision tree of depth one, a single split. AdaBoost's weak learner.
Sample weight
A weight per record, \(1/n\) at the start; the weights always sum to 1 after normalizing.
Total error (TE)
The sum of the sample weights of the records a stump misclassifies.
Performance of stump (α)
\(\tfrac12\ln\frac{1-\mathit{TE}}{\mathit{TE}}\): large when \(\mathit{TE}\) is small, 0 at \(\mathit{TE} = 0.5\).
Buckets
Ranges on [0, 1) as wide as the normalized weights; random numbers falling in them pick the next stump's records.
White box and black box
Whether you can follow how a model reaches its prediction (linear regression, a tree) or not (a forest, a neural network).

Test yourself

Q1How do bagging and boosting differ in how each model gets its data and how the models are combined?

In bagging the models are independent and trained in parallel, each on its own row sample, and they are combined by majority vote (classification) or the mean (regression). In boosting the models form a sequence: each is trained on data that favours the records the previous model got wrong, and the weak learners together make one strong learner.

Q2Five bagged classifiers output 1, 0, 0, 1, 0, and four bagged regressors output 10, 12, 11, 15. What do the two ensembles predict?

The classifier ensemble predicts 0, because three of the five votes are 0. The regressor ensemble predicts the mean, \((10 + 12 + 11 + 15)/4 = 12\).

Q3Why does a random forest use many fully grown trees instead of one, and what happens to bias and variance?

One fully grown tree overfits: low bias, high variance. Trees trained on different rows and features overfit in different ways, so in the vote (or average) their individual errors largely cancel. The forest keeps the low bias and has much lower variance.

Q4Do you need to standardize the features before training a random forest? Before KNN? Why?

Not for a random forest: each split compares one feature with a threshold, and rescaling the feature moves the threshold with it, so the same rows go each way. Yes for KNN: it uses Euclidean or Manhattan distance, and a feature with a large range would dominate the distance.

Q510 records start with equal weights and the first stump misclassifies 2 of them. Find TE, α, the updated weights and the normalized weights.

Each weight is 0.1, so \(\mathit{TE} = 0.2\) and \(\alpha = \tfrac12\ln(0.8/0.2) = \tfrac12\ln 4 = \ln 2 \approx 0.693\). Correct records: \(0.1 \cdot e^{-0.693} = 0.05\). Wrong records: \(0.1 \cdot e^{0.693} = 0.2\). The sum is \(8 \times 0.05 + 2 \times 0.2 = 0.8\), so the normalized weights are \(0.05/0.8 = 0.0625\) and \(0.2/0.8 = 0.25\). The two wrong records hold half the weight.

Q6What is α for a stump with TE = 0.5, and what does that mean?

\(\alpha = \tfrac12\ln(0.5/0.5) = \tfrac12\ln 1 = 0\). The stump is no better than random guessing. Its multipliers are \(e^{0} = 1\), so the weights do not change, and in a weighted vote it gets no say.

Q7With the normalized weights from Q5, how wide are the buckets, and what is the chance that one random draw picks a misclassified record?

Eight buckets are 0.0625 wide and two are 0.25 wide; together they cover 0 to 1. A draw lands in one of the two wide buckets with probability \(0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5\).

Q8Why must the weights be normalized before the buckets are built?

The buckets have to cover the interval from 0 to 1 exactly, so that every random number between 0 and 1 lands in one bucket. That needs the weights to sum to 1. With Krish's unnormalized weights the buckets would only reach 0.649, and draws above that would pick no record.
