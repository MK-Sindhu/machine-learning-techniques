At a glance

  • Naive Bayes is a classification algorithm built on Bayes' theorem, \(P(B \mid A) = P(B)\,P(A \mid B) / P(A)\), which follows from writing \(P(A \text{ and } B)\) in two orders.
  • The naive step: treat the features as independent given the class, so \(P(x_1, \dots, x_n \mid y)\) becomes a product of one-feature terms \(P(x_j \mid y)\) that you can count from a table.
  • The denominator is the same for every class, so compare \(P(y)\prod_j P(x_j \mid y)\) and normalize at the end. On the play-tennis table a sunny, hot day gives 27% yes and 73% no.
  • KNN predicts from the K closest training points: a majority vote for classification, an average for regression. Closeness is Euclidean or Manhattan distance.
  • K is a hyperparameter: try a range (he suggests 1 to 50) and keep the K with the lowest error. KNN works badly with outliers and imbalanced data.

Independent and dependent events

Naive Bayes is a classification algorithm, and it rests on Bayes' theorem. To build up to the theorem, Krish first separates two kinds of events.

Independent events. Roll a die. The probability of a 1 is \(1/6\), and so is the probability of a 2 or a 3. Getting a 1 on one roll has no effect on what the next roll shows, so the rolls are independent.

Dependent events. A bag holds 3 red and 2 green marbles. Take one out: the probability that it is red is \(3/5\). Keep it out and draw again. Only 4 marbles are left, 2 of them green, so the probability of green is now \(2/4 = 1/2\). The first draw changed the bag, so the second event depends on the first.

The probability of red and then green multiplies the two steps. The second factor is a conditional probability: the probability of green given that red has already happened.

\[ P(R \text{ and } G) = P(R)\,P(G \mid R) = \frac{3}{5} \cdot \frac{2}{4} = \frac{3}{10} \]

At one point the audio calls the two marble draws independent. From everything around it he means dependent.

Deriving Bayes' theorem

Write the marble rule for any two events \(A\) and \(B\): the probability of both is the probability of the first times the probability of the second given the first.

\[ P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A) \]

The order of the two events does not matter, so \(P(A \text{ and } B) = P(B \text{ and } A)\). Expanding the right-hand side the same way gives \(P(B)\,P(A \mid B)\). Set the two expansions equal and divide by \(P(A)\):

\[ P(A)\,P(B \mid A) = P(B)\,P(A \mid B) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(B)\,P(A \mid B)}{P(A)} \]
Bayes' theorem \[ P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(B)\,P(A \mid B)}{P(A)} \]

He calls this the crux of Naive Bayes. In the next step \(A\) becomes the input features and \(B\) the output \(y\).

The spoken derivation trips over a few terms, at one point dividing where it should multiply. The lines above are the standard derivation he is building. Not in the video: the usual names are prior for \(P(B)\), likelihood for \(P(A \mid B)\), evidence for \(P(A)\) and posterior for \(P(B \mid A)\).

From Bayes' theorem to Naive Bayes

A training set has independent features \(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\) and an output \(y\), the dependent feature. The model is trained on rows where both are known; afterwards it must predict \(y\) from the inputs alone. Put the features in place of \(A\) and the output in place of \(B\):

\[ P(y \mid x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) = \frac{P(y)\,P(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n \mid y)}{P(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)} \]

The joint term \(P(x_1, \dots, x_n \mid y)\) is hard to estimate. Naive Bayes treats the features as independent of each other once \(y\) is fixed, so the term splits into one factor per feature. He expands the denominator the same way:

\[ P(y \mid x_1, \dots, x_n) = \frac{P(y)\,P(x_1 \mid y)\,P(x_2 \mid y) \cdots P(x_n \mid y)}{P(x_1)\,P(x_2) \cdots P(x_n)} \]

The output \(y\) can be binary (yes or no) or have several classes; the formula is the same.

A small correction: strictly, the denominator is the joint \(P(x_1, \dots, x_n)\), and writing it as \(P(x_1) \cdots P(x_n)\) needs the features to be independent without conditioning on \(y\) too. It makes no difference here, because the next step drops the denominator. Not in the video: this conditional-independence assumption is what the word "naive" refers to.

For the IIT Madras treatment, see Part 14 (part-14.html), which derives the same factorization from a generative story.

Score each class, drop the denominator, normalize

Take a data set with four features and an output that is yes or no. For a record \(\mathbf{x}_i = (x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4)\) the answer could be either class, so we need both probabilities:

\[ P(\text{yes} \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \frac{P(\text{yes})\,P(x_1 \mid \text{yes})\,P(x_2 \mid \text{yes})\,P(x_3 \mid \text{yes})\,P(x_4 \mid \text{yes})}{P(x_1)\,P(x_2)\,P(x_3)\,P(x_4)} \] \[ P(\text{no} \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \frac{P(\text{no})\,P(x_1 \mid \text{no})\,P(x_2 \mid \text{no})\,P(x_3 \mid \text{no})\,P(x_4 \mid \text{no})}{P(x_1)\,P(x_2)\,P(x_3)\,P(x_4)} \]

The denominator is identical in both lines. It is a constant for this record, so it cannot change which class wins, and he drops it. What is left is a score per class:

Naive Bayes decision rule \[ P(y \mid \mathbf{x}) \;\propto\; P(y)\prod_{j=1}^{n} P(x_j \mid y), \qquad \hat y = \text{the class with the larger score} \]

The scores are not probabilities, because the dropped denominator was what made them add up to 1. Suppose a record scores 0.13 for yes and 0.05 for no. Neither number can be compared with the usual 0.5 cut-off. So he normalizes: divide each score by the sum of both.

\[ P(\text{yes} \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \frac{0.13}{0.13 + 0.05} \approx 0.72, \qquad P(\text{no} \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = 1 - 0.72 = 0.28 \]

The record is predicted yes, with 72% probability.

  1. Naive Bayes by hand, as he does it
  2. For each class, count its rows: \(P(y) = \) rows in class \(y\) / all rows.
  3. For each feature, build a table of counts per value and class, and divide by the class size: \(P(x_j = v \mid y)\).
  4. For a new record, multiply the prior by the matching entry of each table, once per class.
  5. Normalize the scores so they sum to 1, and predict the class with the larger value.

Predicting for a sunny, hot day

A new test day arrives: the outlook is sunny and the temperature is hot. Will the person play? Write both scores, leaving out the shared denominator \(P(\text{sunny})\,P(\text{hot})\):

\[ P(\text{yes} \mid \text{sunny}, \text{hot}) \propto P(\text{yes})\,P(\text{sunny} \mid \text{yes})\,P(\text{hot} \mid \text{yes}) = \frac{9}{14} \cdot \frac{2}{9} \cdot \frac{2}{9} = \frac{2}{63} \approx 0.031 \] \[ P(\text{no} \mid \text{sunny}, \text{hot}) \propto P(\text{no})\,P(\text{sunny} \mid \text{no})\,P(\text{hot} \mid \text{no}) = \frac{5}{14} \cdot \frac{3}{5} \cdot \frac{2}{5} = \frac{3}{35} \approx 0.086 \]

Then normalize, exactly as with 0.13 and 0.05:

\[ P(\text{no} \mid \text{sunny}, \text{hot}) = \frac{0.086}{0.031 + 0.086} \approx 0.73, \qquad P(\text{yes} \mid \text{sunny}, \text{hot}) \approx 1 - 0.73 = 0.27 \]

His exercise, a zero count, and the variants

He ends the example with homework: find the prediction for an overcast, mild day. He also points out that you can add more features the same way, building a count table for Humidity and Wind and multiplying in one more factor for each.

Working the exercise with his two tables:

\[ \text{yes: } \frac{9}{14} \cdot \frac{4}{9} \cdot \frac{4}{9} = \frac{8}{63} \approx 0.127, \qquad \text{no: } \frac{5}{14} \cdot \frac{0}{5} \cdot \frac{2}{5} = 0 \]

Normalizing gives 100% yes. No overcast day in the table was a no day, and that single zero count wipes out the whole no score however the other features look. Try it in the calculator above.

Not in the video: this is the zero-frequency problem. The usual fix is Laplace (add-one) smoothing: add 1 to every count and add the number of possible values \(m_j\) to the class total, so no likelihood is ever exactly 0. With it, the overcast, mild day comes out about 87% yes. The calculator's checkbox applies this rule.

\[ P(x_j = v \mid y) = \frac{\text{count}(x_j = v \text{ and } y) + 1}{\text{count}(y) + m_j}, \qquad \text{e.g. } P(\text{overcast} \mid \text{no}) = \frac{0 + 1}{5 + 3} = \frac{1}{8} \]

KNN for classification: a vote among neighbours

The second algorithm of the session is KNN, short for K-nearest neighbours. It is simple, and it solves both classification and regression.

Start with binary classification: two groups of training points, say red and white, and a new point that lands between them. Logistic regression (Part 2) would draw a dividing line. KNN draws nothing. It looks at the training points closest to the new one and lets them decide.

With \(K = 5\), find the five training points nearest the new point. In his picture three of them are red and two are white. Red has the majority, so the new point is classified red.

  1. KNN classification
  2. Choose \(K\) and a distance (Euclidean or Manhattan).
  3. For the new point, compute its distance to every training point.
  4. Take the \(K\) training points with the smallest distances.
  5. Predict the class that appears most often among those \(K\).
3:17:09 3:17:44 the formulas 3:19:40 Manhattan with absolute values

Measuring "nearest": Euclidean and Manhattan distance

"Nearest" needs a distance. He names the two used most often. For two points \((x_1, y_1)\) and \((x_2, y_2)\) (here \(x\) and \(y\) are just the two coordinates of a point):

\[ d_\text{Euclidean} = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}, \qquad d_\text{Manhattan} = \lvert x_2 - x_1 \rvert + \lvert y_2 - y_1 \rvert \]

Euclidean distance is the straight line between the points, the hypotenuse of the right triangle they form. Manhattan distance walks along the axes instead: across, then up, like moving along city blocks. It never takes the hypotenuse. He gives the Manhattan formula with absolute values at the very end of the KNN chapter.

Not in the video: with \(d\) features the same formulas sum over all coordinates, \(\sqrt{\sum_j (a_j - b_j)^2}\) and \(\sum_j \lvert a_j - b_j \rvert\). Both are the Minkowski distance \(\big(\sum_j \lvert a_j - b_j \rvert^p\big)^{1/p}\) with \(p = 2\) and \(p = 1\).

3:18:17

KNN for regression: average the neighbours

Regression works the same way up to the last step. For a new point, find its \(K\) nearest training points (again \(K = 5\) in his example) and predict the average of their output values.

\[ \hat y = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{i \,\in\, N_K(\mathbf{x})} y_i \qquad N_K(\mathbf{x}) = \text{the } K \text{ training points nearest } \mathbf{x} \]

That is the only difference between the two uses: a majority vote for classes, a mean for numbers.

3:18:53

Choosing K

\(K\) is a hyperparameter: we set it, the algorithm does not learn it. His recipe is to try many values, for example \(K = 1\) to \(50\), measure the error rate for each, and keep the model whose error rate is lowest.

Not in the video: one way to run that search in scikit-learn

from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score

error_rate = []
for k in range(1, 51):                       # try K = 1 ... 50
    knn = KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=k, metric="euclidean")   # or "manhattan"
    accuracy = cross_val_score(knn, X_train, y_train, cv=5).mean()
    error_rate.append(1 - accuracy)               # error rate for this K

best_k = error_rate.index(min(error_rate)) + 1    # list index 0 holds K = 1
print("best K:", best_k)
# for regression, KNeighborsRegressor(n_neighbors=best_k) averages the targets

Where KNN goes wrong: outliers and imbalanced data

He names two situations in which KNN works badly.

  • Outliers. Suppose one point of class A sits deep inside the group of class B. A new point next to it is surrounded by B points, yet its nearest neighbour is the outlier, so it is put in class A.
  • Imbalanced data. He names this one without an example. Not in the video: when one class has many more points than the other, it fills most neighbourhoods, and once \(K\) is more than a few, the majority class wins the vote even next to a small group of the other class.

Not in the video: because everything rests on distances, features on large scales (salary in rupees next to age in years) dominate the vote, so features are usually standardized before KNN. Part 13 also notes that prediction is slow, since every new point needs its distance to every training point (part-13.html).

Any nested if-else like this can be drawn as a tree. The first condition becomes the root node, with a yes branch and a no branch. The yes branch ends in a leaf node, "college". The no branch leads to the next condition, which splits again into the leaves "work" and "retire".

Key terms

Independent events
One outcome does not change the probability of the other, as with two rolls of a die.
Dependent events
The first outcome changes the odds of the second, as when marbles are drawn without putting them back.
Conditional probability
\(P(B \mid A)\), the probability of \(B\) once \(A\) has happened; \(P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A)\).
Bayes' theorem
\(P(B \mid A) = P(B)\,P(A \mid B) / P(A)\), from writing \(P(A \text{ and } B)\) in both orders.
Naive Bayes
A classifier that scores each class by \(P(y)\prod_j P(x_j \mid y)\), treating features as independent given the class.
Prior \(P(y)\)
The share of training rows in class \(y\), such as \(9/14\) for yes.
Likelihood \(P(x_j \mid y)\)
The share of class-\(y\) rows with that feature value, such as \(P(\text{sunny} \mid \text{yes}) = 2/9\).
Normalization
Dividing each class score by the sum of the scores so the results add up to 1.
Zero-frequency problem
A value never seen with a class zeroes that class's product; add-one smoothing (not in the video) avoids it.
KNN
Predicts from the K closest training points: majority vote for classification, mean for regression.
Euclidean distance
Straight-line distance, \(\sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}\).
Manhattan distance
Distance along the axes, \(\lvert x_2 - x_1 \rvert + \lvert y_2 - y_1 \rvert\).
Hyperparameter K
The number of neighbours; chosen by trying a range of values and keeping the one with the lowest error.
Root and leaf nodes
The first condition of a decision tree, and the end nodes that give the answer.

Test yourself

Q1From the bag of 3 red and 2 green marbles, you draw two without replacement. What is the probability of green first, then red, and why are these dependent events?

\(P(G) = 2/5\). One green is now gone, leaving 3 red out of 4, so \(P(R \mid G) = 3/4\). The product is \(2/5 \times 3/4 = 6/20 = 0.3\). The events are dependent because the first draw changes what is in the bag for the second.

Q2Derive Bayes' theorem from the rule for \(P(A \text{ and } B)\).

\(P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A)\), and the same event in the other order is \(P(B)\,P(A \mid B)\). Setting them equal and dividing by \(P(A)\) gives \(P(B \mid A) = P(B)\,P(A \mid B)/P(A)\).

Q3Why can Naive Bayes ignore the denominator, and how do you turn scores of 0.02 (yes) and 0.06 (no) into probabilities?

The denominator depends only on the record's features, so it is the same for every class and cannot change which class wins. Normalize by the sum: \(P(\text{yes}) = 0.02/0.08 = 0.25\) and \(P(\text{no}) = 0.75\), so predict no.

Q4Using the Outlook and Temperature tables, predict for a rainy, cool day.

Yes: \(\frac{9}{14} \cdot \frac{3}{9} \cdot \frac{3}{9} = \frac{1}{14} \approx 0.071\). No: \(\frac{5}{14} \cdot \frac{2}{5} \cdot \frac{1}{5} = \frac{1}{35} \approx 0.029\). Normalized, \(P(\text{yes}) = 0.071/0.100 \approx 0.71\), so the person plays (about 71%).

Q5His exercise: overcast and mild. What does Naive Bayes predict, and what is odd about the answer?

Yes: \(\frac{9}{14} \cdot \frac{4}{9} \cdot \frac{4}{9} = \frac{8}{63} \approx 0.127\). No: \(\frac{5}{14} \cdot \frac{0}{5} \cdot \frac{2}{5} = 0\). So it predicts yes with 100% certainty, because no overcast day in the table was a no day. One zero count wipes out the whole no score. Add-one smoothing (not in the video) gives about 87% yes instead.

Q6Find the Euclidean and Manhattan distances between \((1, 2)\) and \((7, 10)\).

The differences are 6 and 8. Euclidean: \(\sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{100} = 10\). Manhattan: \(6 + 8 = 14\). Manhattan is never smaller than Euclidean, since walking along the axes is never shorter than the straight line.

Q7In KNN regression with \(K = 5\), the five nearest neighbours have targets 10, 12, 11, 13 and 54. What is the prediction, and which weakness does this show?

The mean is \((10 + 12 + 11 + 13 + 54)/5 = 100/5 = 20\), far from the 10 to 13 that four of the neighbours agree on. The single outlier, 54, drags the prediction. This is KNN's sensitivity to outliers.

Q8How does he suggest choosing K, and why is \(K = 1\) risky?

Treat K as a hyperparameter: try a range such as 1 to 50, compute the error rate for each, and keep the K with the lowest error. With \(K = 1\) the prediction copies the single nearest point, so one outlier or mislabelled point decides the answer for everything around it.
