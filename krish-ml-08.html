At a glance

  • In Krish's usage, bias is judged on the training data and variance on the test data. Good training accuracy means low bias, good test accuracy means low variance, and low bias with low variance is the generalized model to aim for.
  • XGBoost (extreme gradient boosting) starts from a dummy base model (probability 0.5 for classification, the mean target for regression) and adds binary decision trees one after another, each built on the residuals.
  • Every node gets a similarity weight, \((\sum r)^2 / (\sum p(1-p) + \lambda)\) for classification and \((\sum r)^2 / (n + \lambda)\) for regression. A split's gain is left + right − parent, and the split with the highest gain is used.
  • A prediction adds learning-rate-scaled tree outputs to the base: \(\sigma(0 + \alpha_1 T_1(\mathbf{x}) + \alpha_2 T_2(\mathbf{x}) + \dots)\) for the classifier, \(51 + \alpha_1 T_1(\mathbf{x}) + \dots\) in his regression example. \(\lambda\) is a hyperparameter against overfitting, chosen by cross-validation.
  • An SVM places a hyperplane \(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b = 0\) between marginal planes \(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b = \pm 1\) and maximizes their distance \(2/\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert\). A soft margin accepts some errors through \(C\) and slack terms; a kernel lifts the data to a higher dimension where a plane can split it.

What bias and variance mean

Krish opens with a question students often ask him. A model reaches 90% accuracy on the training data and 70% on the test data. Most people call that overfitting and describe overfitting as low bias and high variance. Why is bias tied to the training data, and variance to the test data? He answers from the definitions.

Bias

He defines bias as a phenomenon that "skews the result of an algorithm in favor or against an idea". Here the idea is the training data set. A model trained on it can come out in its favour, performing well on it, or against it, performing poorly.

  • Performs well on the training data: low bias.
  • Performs poorly on the training data: high bias.

As captioned, his first statement swaps the two labels: a model that does well on the training data is called high bias. His examples a few minutes later use the convention above, which is also the standard one, so the notes follow it.

Variance

Variance, in his definition, refers to the changes in the model when it is used on a different portion of the data: other training data, or the test data. The data set is split into a training part and a test part. The model learns on the training part, which is where bias is judged, and then predicts on the test part.

  • Good predictions on the test data: low variance.
  • Poor predictions on the test data: high variance.

Not in the video: in the textbook sense, variance measures how much the fitted model would change if it were trained on a different sample of data, and bias measures how far its average prediction sits from the truth. A large gap between training and test accuracy is the usual symptom of high variance. For the IIT Madras treatment, see Part 17 (part-17.html).

5:47:22

Three steps per tree

  1. Building one XGBoost tree (his steps)
  2. Create a binary decision tree from the features. Every split has exactly two branches, even for a feature with three categories.
  3. Calculate the similarity weight of each node.
  4. Calculate the gain of each candidate split and keep the split with the highest gain.
\[ \text{Similarity weight} = \frac{\big(\sum_i r_i\big)^2}{\sum_i p_i\,(1 - p_i) + \lambda} \]

The sums run over the records in the node. \(r_i\) is a record's residual and \(p_i\) the probability the model so far gives it, 0.5 for every record in the first tree. \(\lambda\) is a hyperparameter that keeps the tree from overfitting. He first says it starts at 1, then sets \(\lambda = 0\) for the worked example.

Read the numerator carefully: add the residuals first, then square the total once. He corrects himself on exactly this while working the example. Residuals of opposite sign cancel, so a node whose records disagree gets a low similarity weight, and a node whose residuals all point the same way gets a high one.

\[ \text{Gain} = \text{SW}_{\text{left}} + \text{SW}_{\text{right}} - \text{SW}_{\text{parent}} \]

He calls this the information gain, borrowing the decision-tree term (krish-ml-05.html); XGBoost's documentation calls it the gain.

5:48:32 5:51:09 similarity weights 5:57:11 gain

Worked example: split on salary

Split the root on salary. Records 1, 2, 3 and 7 go left (≤ 50K); records 4, 5 and 6 go right (> 50K). Every record has \(p = 0.5\), so each adds \(0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25\) to a denominator. With \(\lambda = 0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{SW}_{\le 50K} &= \frac{(-0.5 + 0.5 + 0.5 - 0.5)^2}{4 \times 0.25} = \frac{0}{1} = 0 \\ \text{SW}_{> 50K} &= \frac{(-0.5 + 0.5 + 0.5)^2}{3 \times 0.25} = \frac{0.25}{0.75} = 0.33 \\ \text{SW}_{\text{root}} &= \frac{(3 \times (-0.5) + 4 \times 0.5)^2}{7 \times 0.25} = \frac{0.25}{1.75} = 0.14 \end{aligned} \]

The left node holds two approvals and two rejections, so its residuals cancel exactly. The root has one more approval than rejection, so its weight is small but above zero.

Gain of the salary split \[ \text{Gain} = 0 + 0.33 - 0.14 = 0.19 \]

The feature whose split gives the highest gain is chosen for the node. He assumes salary wins here and carries on with it.

Not in the video: with these seven records salary does not win. Splitting the root on credit, good against bad or normal, gives a gain of 3.86, and bad against the rest gives 3.66. The calculator further down lists every candidate. The procedure is the same whichever split wins, so the notes follow his tree.

5:58:18

Splitting further on credit

Next he splits the salary ≤ 50K branch on credit. Credit has three categories and an XGBoost tree only makes binary splits, so the categories are grouped two against one. He puts good and normal on one side and bad on the other. Record 1 goes to "bad"; records 2, 3 and 7 go to "good or normal".

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{SW}_{\text{bad}} &= \frac{(-0.5)^2}{0.25} = 1 \\ \text{SW}_{\text{good, normal}} &= \frac{(0.5 + 0.5 - 0.5)^2}{3 \times 0.25} = 0.33 \\ \text{Gain} &= 1 + 0.33 - 0 = 1.33 \end{aligned} \]

The parent here is the ≤ 50K node, whose similarity weight is 0, so nothing is subtracted. The other groupings (good against bad and normal, normal against the rest) are scored the same way, and the one with the highest gain is kept. The > 50K branch could be split in the same way; he leaves it.

6:02:10 6:04:20 adding trees

From trees to a probability

To predict for a record, start at the base model. Its probability 0.5 is first turned into log-odds:

\[ \log\frac{p}{1 - p} = \log\frac{0.5}{0.5} = \log 1 = 0 \]

The record then goes down the tree. Record 1 (≤ 50K, bad) ends in the "bad" leaf. The leaf's value is multiplied by a learning rate \(\alpha\), a small number, and added to the base's 0. Because this is classification, a sigmoid turns the total into a probability between 0 and 1.

Correction: in the video the leaf contributes its similarity weight (1 for the "bad" leaf). In XGBoost a leaf contributes its output value, the sum of its residuals over the same denominator, with no square. A similarity weight is never negative, so it could never pull record 1 toward rejection; the output value keeps the residuals' sign.

\[ \text{output} = \frac{\sum_i r_i}{\sum_i p_i(1 - p_i) + \lambda} \]

For his tree with \(\lambda = 0\), the "bad" leaf gives \(-0.5/0.25 = -2\) and the "good or normal" leaf \(0.5/0.75 = 0.67\).

More trees are then added one after another, each with its own learning-rate term, until the combination becomes a strong learner:

XGBoost classifier output \[ p(\mathbf{x}) = \sigma\big(0 + \alpha_1 T_1(\mathbf{x}) + \alpha_2 T_2(\mathbf{x}) + \dots + \alpha_n T_n(\mathbf{x})\big) \]

\(T_t(\mathbf{x})\) is the output of tree \(t\) for the record and \(\sigma\) the sigmoid. Adding the tree outputs a little at a time is what makes it boosting.

Not in the video: XGBoost uses one learning rate for every tree (learning_rate, also called eta, 0.3 by default). Before tree \(t+1\) is built, each record's probability becomes \(\sigma\) of its running total and the residuals are recomputed from it. From the second tree on, the \(p_i(1 - p_i)\) terms in the similarity weight differ from record to record.

6:05:23 6:07:06 λ by cross-validation

Black box, overfitting and λ

Working these numbers by hand for many trees is impractical, so he calls XGBoost a black-box model. Asked whether it is prone to overfitting, he says it needs hyperparameter tuning, the pre-pruning used for decision trees. \(\lambda\) is one of those hyperparameters, and it is set with cross-validation.

\(\lambda\) sits in the denominator of every similarity weight. Raising it shrinks all of them, and it shrinks the weights of nodes with few records the most, because their denominators start small. With \(\lambda = 1\), the one-record "bad" leaf drops from \(0.25/0.25 = 1\) to \(0.25/1.25 = 0.2\), a fifth of its value, while the four-record ≤ 50K node's denominator only doubles. Splits that isolate one or two records lose most of their gain.

Not in the video: cover, γ and where the formulas come from

  • Cover is the denominator without \(\lambda\): \(\sum p(1-p)\) for classification, the number of records for regression. The setting min_child_weight (default 1) forbids a child whose cover is below it. At \(p = 0.5\) each record adds only 0.25, so the default would block his one-record "bad" leaf.
  • \(\gamma\) (gamma, also min_split_loss, default 0) is the smallest gain a split must reach. Once a tree is grown, splits whose gain falls below \(\gamma\) are pruned from the bottom up. Larger \(\gamma\) gives smaller trees.
  • Both formulas come from a second-order approximation of the loss. With first derivatives \(g_i\) and second derivatives \(h_i\) of the loss, a node's score is \((\sum g_i)^2/(\sum h_i + \lambda)\) and its best output is \(-\sum g_i/(\sum h_i + \lambda)\). For log loss \(g_i = p_i - y_i = -r_i\) and \(h_i = p_i(1-p_i)\); for squared error \(h_i = 1\), which is why the regressor's denominator counts records. The XGBoost paper also puts a factor ½ in front of the gain, which does not change which split wins. \(\lambda\) acts as an L2 penalty on leaf outputs, the same idea as ridge regression (Part 12, krish-ml-02.html).

6:09:50 6:10:53 similarity weight 6:13:34 gain

Similarity weight for regression, and a first split

Experience is continuous, so candidate thresholds are taken between sorted values, as for a decision tree regressor (krish-ml-05.html). The first candidate is experience ≤ 2 against > 2: one residual goes left and four go right.

For regression the similarity weight changes. The denominator counts residuals instead of summing \(p(1-p)\):

Similarity weight, regression \[ \text{SW} = \frac{\big(\sum_i r_i\big)^2}{n + \lambda} \]

\(n\) is the number of residuals in the node. The larger \(\lambda\), the more the residuals are penalized.

With \(\lambda = 0\) the left node would score \(121/1 = 121\). He then sets \(\lambda = 1\) (he calls it alpha at this point) to show the penalty:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{SW}_{\le 2} &= \frac{(-11)^2}{1 + 1} = \frac{121}{2} = 60.5 \\ \text{SW}_{> 2} &= \frac{(-9 + 1 + 9 + 11)^2}{4 + 1} = \frac{144}{5} = 28.8 \\ \text{SW}_{\text{root}} &= \frac{(-11 - 9 + 1 + 9 + 11)^2}{5 + 1} = \frac{1}{6} = 0.17 \\ \text{Gain} &= 60.5 + 28.8 - 0.17 = 89.13 \end{aligned} \]

He first reads \(121/2\) as 65.5 and \(144/5\) as 28.5, and corrects both to 60.5 and 28.8 a moment later. The gain 89.13 is his final figure.

6:14:44

A better split at 2.5 years

The next threshold, experience ≤ 2.5 against > 2.5, puts the two negative residuals on one side and the three positive ones on the other.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{SW}_{\le 2.5} &= \frac{(-11 - 9)^2}{2 + 1} = \frac{400}{3} = 133.33 \\ \text{SW}_{> 2.5} &= \frac{(1 + 9 + 11)^2}{3 + 1} = \frac{441}{4} = 110.25 \\ \text{Gain} &= 133.33 + 110.25 - 0.17 = 243.42 \end{aligned} \]

He only remarks that this is clearly larger than 89.13; the value 243.42 is worked out for these notes. The split with the higher gain is used, so experience ≤ 2.5 becomes the first split, and further splits are found the same way. 6:17:33

Predicting a salary

A new record goes to the base model first and gets 51. It then goes down the tree, and the leaf it reaches contributes the average of its residuals, scaled by the learning rate:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{experience} \le 2.5: &\quad \hat y = 51 + \alpha_1 \cdot \frac{-11 - 9}{2} = 51 - 10\,\alpha_1 \\ \text{experience} > 2.5: &\quad \hat y = 51 + \alpha_1 \cdot \frac{1 + 9 + 11}{3} = 51 + 7\,\alpha_1 \end{aligned} \]

Further trees are built separately and added in the same way:

XGBoost regressor output \[ \hat y(\mathbf{x}) = 51 + \alpha_1 T_1(\mathbf{x}) + \alpha_2 T_2(\mathbf{x}) + \dots + \alpha_n T_n(\mathbf{x}) \]

As with the classifier, he stresses that the result cannot be inspected by eye: in practice it is a black box.

Not in the video: XGBoost's leaf output for regression is \(\sum r / (n + \lambda)\). The plain average he uses is the \(\lambda = 0\) case. With his \(\lambda = 1\) the two leaves would give \(-20/3 = -6.67\) and \(21/4 = 5.25\), a little closer to zero.

6:19:04 6:20:42 hard and soft margin

Hyperplane and marginal planes

He introduces the support vector machine as close to logistic regression. Logistic regression fits a best-fit line that divides two classes of points (krish-ml-02.html). An SVM draws that line, the hyperplane, and also two parallel marginal planes, one on each side, passing through the nearest points of each class. Among all candidate lines, the one whose marginal planes are farthest apart divides the points best.

Real data overlap. If the classes can be split with no point inside or across the marginal planes, the result is a hard margin. When some points land on the wrong side and errors must be allowed, it is a soft margin. He notes that a hard margin is practically impossible on a normal data set, so in practice the SVM builds a soft margin: the widest margin it can get while tolerating some errors, controlled by hyperparameters.

Not in the video: the points the marginal planes rest on, plus any points inside or beyond them in a soft margin, are the support vectors. They alone fix the hyperplane; moving any other point (without crossing a marginal plane) changes nothing. For the IIT Madras treatment, see Part 16 (part-16.html).

6:21:15 6:23:28 which side

The line as \(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b = 0\)

He rewrites the familiar line \(y = mx + c\). The general form \(ax + by + c = 0\) describes the same line; solving it for \(y\) gives

\[ y = -\frac{a}{b}\,x - \frac{c}{b} \]

So the slope is \(m = -a/b\) and the intercept is \(-c/b\).

With features \(x_1, x_2, \dots\) and weights \(w_1, w_2, \dots\) the same form becomes

\[ w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \dots + b = 0 \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b = 0 \]

The sign of \(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b\) tells which side of the line a point is on. His example takes a line through the origin (\(b = 0\)) with slope −1, the line \(x_2 = -x_1\). In this form it has \(\mathbf{w} = (-1, -1)\):

Point\(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b\)Where it is
\((-4,\ 0)\)\((-1)(-4) + (-1)(0) = 4\)below the line: positive
\((4,\ 4)\)\((-1)(4) + (-1)(4) = -8\)above the line: negative

Every point below this line gives a positive value and every point above it a negative one, so the sign separates the two categories, and a plane can split the points.

His spoken arithmetic multiplies only the first coordinate by −1, which gives −4 for the second point. The full \(\mathbf{w}\) for this line gives −8. The sign, which is all that matters here, is the same.

6:26:35 6:29:23 margin width 6:31:05 constraints

Maximize the margin

The marginal planes are the places where the same expression equals −1 and +1. The plane on the negative side is \(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b = -1\) and the one on the positive side \(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b = +1\). He would rather write \(\pm k\), but most articles and research papers use \(\pm 1\).

Take a point \(\mathbf{x}_1\) on the +1 plane and a point \(\mathbf{x}_2\) on the −1 plane and subtract the two equations; \(b\) cancels:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_1 + b = 1,\quad \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_2 + b = -1 \;\;&\Rightarrow\;\; \mathbf{w}^\top(\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{x}_2) = 2 \\ &\Rightarrow\;\; \frac{\mathbf{w}^\top}{\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert}(\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{x}_2) = \frac{2}{\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert} \end{aligned} \]

Dividing by the length \(\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert\) leaves a unit vector along \(\mathbf{w}\), so the left side is the distance between the two planes. The wider this gap, the better the model, so the SVM's aim is:

Hard-margin SVM, as he writes it \[ \max_{\mathbf{w},\, b} \; \frac{2}{\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert} \quad \text{such that} \quad y_i\,(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i + b) \ge 1 \;\text{ for every point } i \]

The constraint packs two conditions into one. A point with label \(y_i = +1\) must have \(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i + b \ge 1\), and a point with \(y_i = -1\) must have \(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i + b \le -1\). Multiplying by \(y_i\) turns both into \(\ge 1\) for correctly classified points: for a negative point, −1 times a value ≤ −1 is at least 1.

Because optimizers are usually written as minimizations, he flips the fraction 6:33:12. Maximizing \(2/\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert\) is the same as minimizing \(\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert/2\), by adjusting \(\mathbf{w}\) and \(b\).

Not in the video: textbooks minimize \(\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2\), which has the same solution and is smoother to optimize. For the width derivation and the full hard-margin problem, see Part 16 (part-16.html).

6:34:19

Soft margin: C and the slack terms

For data that overlap, he adds two things to the objective:

\[ \min_{\mathbf{w},\, b} \; \frac{\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert}{2} + C \sum_{i=1}^{n} \xi_i \]
  • \(C\) (he writes it with an index, \(C_i\)) says how many errors are acceptable even with the marginal planes in place, for example six or seven.
  • \(\xi_i\) (he calls it eta) is the distance of a wrongly placed point from its marginal plane. Summing over the wrong points of both classes gives the total error.

If the data cannot be fitted cleanly, these two hyperparameters let the SVM accept some errors and still draw the best line with the widest marginal planes it can. The dashed segments in the soft-margin figure above are the \(\xi_i\).

Correction and context: in the standard formulation \(C\) is one constant, the same for every point, and it does not count errors directly. It is the price of each unit of slack. A large \(C\) punishes violations heavily and gives a narrower margin with fewer errors; a small \(C\) accepts more violations in exchange for a wider margin. The constraints change too, to \(y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i + b) \ge 1 - \xi_i\) with \(\xi_i \ge 0\): a point on or beyond its marginal plane has \(\xi_i = 0\), a point inside the margin has \(0 < \xi_i < 1\), and a misclassified point has \(\xi_i > 1\). See Part 16 (slack variables, what C does).

6:36:32 6:37:04 kernels

SVR and kernels

For support vector regression (SVR) he says only one part of this formulation changes, and leaves finding out which as an assignment.

Not in the video: SVR keeps \(\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + C\sum(\text{slack})\) and changes the constraints. Instead of each point sitting on the correct side of a margin, each target must lie inside a tube of half-width \(\varepsilon\) around the prediction, \(|y_i - (\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i + b)| \le \varepsilon\), with one slack for points above the tube and one for points below. Points inside the tube cost nothing.

Some data cannot be divided by any straight line, for example one class forming a ring around the other. An SVM kernel converts the two-dimensional data into three dimensions, pushing one class up and the other down, and a plane can then split them. For the details he points to his separate video on SVM kernels, which includes a practical demonstration.

Key terms

Bias (his usage)
How well a model does on the data it was trained on. Good training accuracy means low bias.
Variance (his usage)
How well the model holds up on other data, such as the test set. Good test accuracy means low variance.
Overfitting
Good on the training data, poor on the test data: low bias, high variance.
Generalized model
Low bias and low variance: accurate on training and test data alike. The aim.
XGBoost
Extreme gradient boosting: binary decision trees added one after another to a base model, each built on the current residuals.
Base model
The starting prediction: probability 0.5 (log-odds 0) for classification, the mean target for regression.
Residual
Target minus current prediction; what the next tree tries to explain.
Similarity weight
\((\sum r)^2/(\sum p(1-p) + \lambda)\) for classification, \((\sum r)^2/(n + \lambda)\) for regression. High when a node's residuals agree.
Gain
\(\text{SW}_{\text{left}} + \text{SW}_{\text{right}} - \text{SW}_{\text{parent}}\); the split with the highest gain is used.
\(\lambda\) (lambda)
Hyperparameter added to every denominator. Larger \(\lambda\) shrinks similarity weights and outputs, which fights overfitting. Chosen by cross-validation.
Learning rate \(\alpha\)
Factor that scales each tree's output before it is added, so the model improves in small steps.
Hyperplane and marginal planes
\(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b = 0\) separates the classes; \(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b = \pm1\) pass through the nearest points. Their distance is \(2/\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert\).
Soft margin
A margin that allows errors. The slack \(\xi_i\) measures each violation and \(C\) sets its price.
SVM kernel
A mapping to a higher dimension where a plane can separate classes that no straight line separates.

Test yourself

Q1A model scores 95% on the training data and 60% on the test data. Describe it in Krish's terms.

Training accuracy is good, so low bias. Test accuracy is poor, so high variance. Low bias with high variance is overfitting.

Q2A classifier node holds residuals −0.5, −0.5 and +0.5, all from \(p = 0.5\). Find its similarity weight with \(\lambda = 0\) and with \(\lambda = 1\).

\(\sum r = -0.5\), squared 0.25. Cover \(= 3 \times 0.25 = 0.75\). With \(\lambda = 0\): \(0.25/0.75 = 0.33\). With \(\lambda = 1\): \(0.25/1.75 = 0.14\).

Q3Why is the similarity weight of the salary ≤ 50K node exactly 0, and what does that say about the node?

It holds two approvals (+0.5 each) and two rejections (−0.5 each), so the residuals sum to 0 before squaring. The node does not lean either way; its output value would also be 0.

Q4For the salary data with \(\lambda = 1\), compute the gain of the split experience ≤ 3 against > 3. Does it beat ≤ 2.5?

Q5What happens to similarity weights and gains as \(\lambda\) grows, and which splits suffer most?

Every weight shrinks, because \(\lambda\) is added to every denominator. Nodes with a small cover (few records) shrink proportionally the most, so splits that isolate one or two records lose most of their gain. That makes the tree less eager to chase individual points, which is how \(\lambda\) fights overfitting.

Q7Record 1 lands in the classifier's "bad" leaf (one residual −0.5, \(p = 0.5\), \(\lambda = 0\)). With \(\alpha = 0.3\), what is its probability after the first tree, using XGBoost's leaf output?

Q8An SVM has \(\mathbf{w} = (3, 4)\). How wide is its margin, and what would raising \(C\) do in a soft-margin SVM?

\(\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert = 5\), so the distance between the marginal planes is \(2/5 = 0.4\). A larger \(C\) makes each unit of slack more expensive, so the optimizer accepts fewer violations and usually a narrower margin.
